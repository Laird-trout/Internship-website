    Programs that take first- and second-year students

    Most internship postings say "junior or senior preferred." These are the exceptions: structured programs that either exist specifically for early undergraduates or have a long track record of hiring them. Federal research programs are the deepest pool for sophomores in EE, and they pay a living stipend with housing help.

    September 2026 to August 2027

    Sophomore recruiting is front-loaded: the biggest employers open in August and September and fill on a rolling basis. Federal programs run on fixed deadlines from November to February. Utilities and small firms post last. Dates below are the usual windows; the source of truth is always the posting.

      How a sophomore actually gets an EE internship

      There are fewer sophomore slots, so the game is different from the junior cycle. You win on four things: applying early to the employers that genuinely hire second-years, showing up at career fairs with a project you can talk about for ten minutes, applying in volume without letting quality slip, and treating federal research programs as first-class options rather than backups.

      Build your target list

      • Filter the catalog to Regularly hires sophomores. That is your primary list; apply to all of them.
      • Add Near Corvallis. Regional employers interview Oregon State students at higher rates because they can hire locally and cheaply, and many recruit at the OSU engineering career fairs.
      • Add every federal program you are eligible for (NASA OSTEM, DOE SULI, NIST SURF, NREIP, AFRL Scholars, Sandia, PNNL). They accept students who have completed one year, and the competition from upperclassmen is thinner than in industry.
      • Pick ten "reach" employers you would love regardless of odds. Apply, then forget about them.

      Resume that works for an ECE sophomore

      One page. Recruiters at a fair spend under twenty seconds on it. Order the sections by what you have the most of: Education, Skills, Projects, Experience, Activities. For a sophomore, Projects usually outranks Experience, so put it higher.

      • Education: Oregon State University, B.S. Electrical and Computer Engineering, expected 2029. GPA if 3.3 or above. Relevant coursework as a single line (circuits, digital logic, programming, signals).
      • Skills: group by type, not as one list. Hardware: oscilloscope, DMM, function generator, soldering, breadboarding. Design tools: KiCad or Altium, LTspice, MATLAB. Languages: C, Python, Verilog or VHDL, assembly. Platforms: Arduino, STM32, ESP32, Raspberry Pi, FPGA dev board.
      • Projects: three to five, each with two or three bullets. Use the formula did X using Y, achieving Z. "Designed a two-layer PCB in KiCad for a 3.3 V sensor node; verified rails and I2C traffic with an oscilloscope; reduced idle draw to 8 mA."
      • Experience: any job counts. Retail, lifeguarding, tutoring. Frame it around responsibility and reliability, one or two bullets each.
      • Cut objective statements, references, high-school awards, and soft-skill lists. Put a GitHub link and a project portfolio link in the header.

      Projects that convert into interviews

      Interviewers hire sophomores on evidence of hands-on ability and curiosity. The project does not need to be novel; it needs to be finished, documented, and understood deeply.

      • A custom PCB you designed, ordered, assembled, and debugged. Even a simple one. Being able to say "I've had a board fabricated" separates you from most sophomores.
      • An embedded system with real sensors and a real constraint (battery life, timing, noise). Talk about the bug that took longest.
      • An FPGA design in Verilog or VHDL: a UART, a VGA controller, a simple CPU. Know your timing diagrams.
      • A power or motor project: a buck converter, an H-bridge motor driver, a battery charger. Measured efficiency is a great talking point.
      • An instrument or RF build: a metal detector, an antenna you measured, a software-defined-radio receiver, a signal generator. Anything where you had to measure a physical signal and reason about noise.
      • Document each project with a short write-up, a schematic, a photo, and the code on GitHub. Link them from your portfolio site.

      Career fair script

      Oregon State runs engineering career fairs in fall and winter (check the Career Development Center calendar; fall is typically October and winter is typically February). Go to both. Research the employer list the week before and pick fifteen tables in priority order.

      1. Open (15 seconds): "Hi, I'm Laird, a sophomore in ECE at Oregon State. I'm looking for a summer 2027 hardware internship, and I'm especially interested in [their thing]."
      2. Proof (30 seconds): one project, told as a problem you hit and how you solved it, with a number in it.
      3. Ask (one question): "Do your intern roles take sophomores?" or "What does your team look for in early-undergrad interns?" Their answer tells you whether to spend more time.
      4. Close: get a name and email, ask if there's a specific req to apply to, and write it down before the next table.
      5. Within 24 hours: send the follow-up email below and apply to the req they named. Mention the fair conversation in the application if there's a field for it.

      Application volume and cadence

      • Target 60 to 100 applications between September and February. Two per weekday is enough.
      • Apply within two weeks of a posting going live. Rolling programs (Lockheed, Tesla, SpaceX, Apple, the defense primes) fill early.
      • Tailor the resume lightly, not heavily: swap which project is first, adjust the skills line. Don't rewrite it per application.
      • Write a cover letter only when required or for your top ten. Three short paragraphs: why them, why you fit, one specific thing you built.
      • Log every application in the tracker on this site with the date. Follow up on silent applications after three weeks with a short email to the recruiter.

      Technical interview checklist (sophomore level)

      Interviewers calibrate to your year. They will probe fundamentals and how you think, not advanced theory.

      • Circuits: KVL and KCL, Thevenin and Norton, voltage dividers, RC and RL time constants, first-order filters, op-amp inverting and non-inverting configurations, ideal-op-amp assumptions, diode and MOSFET basics.
      • Digital: Boolean simplification and K-maps, combinational vs sequential logic, flip-flops and latches, finite state machines, setup and hold time, metastability, what a clock domain is, basic Verilog structure.
      • Embedded: C pointers and bit manipulation, volatile, interrupts vs polling, UART/SPI/I2C differences and when to use each, timers and PWM, ADC resolution and sampling, reading a datasheet register map.
      • Signals: what the Fourier transform tells you, sampling and Nyquist, aliasing, why you low-pass before an ADC.
      • Power: linear regulator vs switching regulator trade-offs, buck converter operation at a block level, why MOSFETs switch losses matter, battery C-rates and basic Li-ion safety.
      • Bench: how to set up an oscilloscope trigger, what probe loading is, how to find a short on a board, how you debugged your own project.
      • Behavioral: prepare five stories in situation, action, result form: a bug you fixed, a team conflict, a deadline miss, a time you learned something fast, a time you were wrong.

      Citizenship and clearances, in plain terms

      • US citizen required means exactly that, usually because the role needs a security clearance or the work is classified. Defense primes and the intelligence community.
      • US person (citizen or permanent resident) is an export-control (ITAR) requirement, common at aerospace companies and national labs. International students on F-1 visas generally cannot fill these roles.
      • Clearance timelines are long. NSA and some DoD roles have deadlines in October or November for the following summer because the background investigation takes months. Apply to those first.
      • If you are an international student, sort by No citizenship requirement. Semiconductor companies, big tech, automotive, medical devices, and most industrial firms sponsor CPT interns routinely.

      If the offer doesn't come

      Plenty of strong engineers land their first internship as juniors. If it's April and nothing has landed: ask an OSU faculty member about paid summer research (many labs hire sophomores who ask in person), look at the OSU URSA Engage program for the following year, take on a paid campus technical job, or spend the summer finishing one ambitious, well-documented project. Any of those makes the junior-year cycle dramatically easier.
